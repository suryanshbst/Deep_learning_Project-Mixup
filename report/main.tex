\begin{abstract}
Standard deep neural networks trained using the Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) principle exhibit severe undesirable behaviors, including memorization of corrupt labels, extreme vulnerability to adversarial perturbations, and poor predictive calibration. In this paper, we present an end-to-end empirical reproduction and theoretical analysis of the landmark ICLR 2018 paper \textit{"mixup: Beyond Empirical Risk Minimization"} by Zhang et al. We investigate the Vicinal Risk Minimization (VRM) principle by training a PreAct ResNet-18 architecture on convex linear combinations of feature vectors and their target encodings, parameterized by a Beta distribution $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$. Adapted to a Google Colab Nvidia T4 GPU compute budget, our evaluation on CIFAR-10 analyzes generalization performance, epoch scaling dynamics (50 vs. 120 epochs), corrupted label memorization (20\% and 50\% symmetric noise), Expected Calibration Error (ECE), and hyperparameter ablation sweeps over $\alpha \in \{0.0, 0.2, 0.5, 1.0, 2.0\}$. We provide a detailed side-by-side comparison between the original authors' full cluster setup (200 epochs on Tesla P100 GPUs) and our resource-constrained reproduction. Our findings confirm that mixup smooths decision boundaries, suppresses corrupt label memorization, and improves network calibration, serving as a highly effective data-agnostic regularizer across compute scales.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction \& Problem Motivation}

Deep neural networks have driven revolutionary advances across computer vision, speech recognition, audio processing, and natural language understanding. In almost all state-of-the-art applications, neural network optimization relies on two foundational paradigms. First, model parameters are trained to minimize average loss over the training dataset, a learning rule known as the Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) principle \cite{b2}. Second, the parameter count of modern deep architectures scales linearly with dataset size, frequently exceeding the number of training examples by several orders of magnitude.

Strikingly, classical statistical learning theory \cite{b3} establishes that the uniform convergence of ERM is guaranteed only when the capacity of the learning machine (measured via Vapnik-Chervonenkis or VC-complexity) remains strictly constrained relative to sample size $n$. Modern overparameterized deep networks fundamentally challenge this classical bound. As demonstrated empirically by Zhang et al. \cite{b4}, ERM enables large networks to fit and memorize training data even when labels are assigned completely at random. Furthermore, neural networks trained via ERM exhibit extreme sensitivity to tiny, visually imperceptible adversarial noise \cite{b5}, changing predictions drastically when evaluated on inputs just outside the training distribution support.

In high-dimensional feature spaces, ERM optimizes decision boundaries that yield sharp transitions immediately adjacent to observed training points. Consequently, models trained under standard ERM suffer from three distinct pathological failure modes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Adversarial Fragility}: Minute, visually imperceptible perturbations constructed via gradient sign methods can flip model predictions with high confidence \cite{b5}.
    \item \textbf{Corrupted Label Memorization}: When training datasets contain mislabeled or noisy samples, deep networks prioritize learning clean patterns initially, but inevitably memorize corrupt instances during later training epochs \cite{b9}.
    \item \textbf{Predictive Overconfidence}: Cross-entropy loss evaluated on discrete one-hot ground-truth encodings drives logit magnitudes toward infinity, producing poorly calibrated predictive probabilities where confidence systematically dwarfs accuracy \cite{b8}.
\end{enumerate}

To address the limitations of ERM, data augmentation routines construct virtual training examples by drawing from a neighborhood or vicinity around training points, formalized as the Vicinal Risk Minimization (VRM) principle \cite{b6}. Standard image augmentations (such as horizontal flips, random cropping, and slight rotations) construct human-engineered vicinities. However, traditional VRM requires extensive expert domain knowledge and operates under the strong assumption that all virtual examples within a vicinity share the exact same ground-truth label, failing to model transitions across distinct class boundaries.

Motivated by these issues, Zhang et al. \cite{b1} introduced \textit{mixup}, a simple, data-agnostic data augmentation routine that constructs virtual training examples through linear interpolation of feature vectors and target vectors:
\begin{align}
\tilde{x} &= \lambda x_i + (1 - \lambda) x_j, \label{eq:mix_x} \\
\tilde{y} &= \lambda y_i + (1 - \lambda) y_j, \label{eq:mix_y}
\end{align}
where $(x_i, y_i)$ and $(x_j, y_j)$ are two feature-target pairs drawn at random from the training dataset, and $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ is drawn from a Beta distribution $\text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ for hyperparameter $\alpha \in (0, \infty)$.

In this paper, we conduct a rigorous, publication-grade reproduction of the mixup principle on CIFAR-10. We detail the mathematical formulations of VRM, describe compute scaling strategies for free-tier Google Colab T4 GPUs, evaluate label noise resistance and model calibration, and present an explicit side-by-side comparative analysis between the original paper's cluster benchmark results and our scaled reproduction numbers across a complete 5-page report.

\section{From Empirical Risk Minimization to Mixup}

\subsection{Expected Risk and Empirical Risk Minimization}
In supervised learning, we are interested in finding a function $f \in \mathcal{F}$ that describes the relationship between a random feature vector $X \in \mathcal{X}$ and a random target vector $Y \in \mathcal{Y}$, which follow the joint distribution $P(X, Y)$. To this end, we define a loss function $\ell$ that penalizes the differences between predictions $f(x)$ and actual targets $y$. Then, we minimize the average of the loss function $\ell$ over the data distribution $P$, also known as the expected risk:
\begin{equation}
R(f) = \int_{\mathcal{X} \times \mathcal{Y}} \ell(f(x), y) \, dP(x, y). \label{eq:expected_risk}
\end{equation}
Unfortunately, the distribution $P$ is unknown in most practical situations. Instead, we usually have access to a set of training data $\mathcal{D} = \{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, where $(x_i, y_i) \sim P$. Using the training data $\mathcal{D}$, we approximate $P$ by the empirical distribution:
\begin{equation}
P_\delta(x, y) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \delta(x = x_i, y = y_i), \label{eq:empirical_dist}
\end{equation}
where $\delta(x = x_i, y = y_i)$ is a Dirac mass centered at $(x_i, y_i)$. Using the empirical distribution $P_\delta$, we approximate the expected risk by the empirical risk:
\begin{equation}
R_\delta(f) = \int \ell(f(x), y) \, dP_\delta(x, y) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \ell(f(x_i), y_i). \label{eq:erm_risk}
\end{equation}
Learning the function $f$ by minimizing \eqref{eq:erm_risk} is known as the Empirical Risk Minimization (ERM) principle. While efficient to compute, the empirical risk monitors the behavior of $f$ only at a finite set of $n$ examples. When considering functions with a number of parameters comparable to $n$ (such as large neural networks), one trivial way to minimize \eqref{eq:erm_risk} is to memorize the training data.

\subsection{Vicinal Risk Minimization (VRM) and Mixup}
In the Vicinal Risk Minimization (VRM) principle \cite{b6}, the distribution $P$ is approximated by:
\begin{equation}
P_\nu(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y}) = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \nu(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y} \mid x_i, y_i), \label{eq:vrm_dist}
\end{equation}
where $\nu$ is a vicinity distribution that measures the probability of finding the virtual feature-target pair $(\tilde{x}, \tilde{y})$ in the vicinity of the training pair $(x_i, y_i)$. To learn using VRM, we sample the vicinal distribution to construct a dataset $\mathcal{D}_\nu = \{(\tilde{x}_i, \tilde{y}_i)\}_{i=1}^m$, and minimize the empirical vicinal risk:
\begin{equation}
R_\nu(f) = \frac{1}{m} \sum_{i=1}^m \ell(f(\tilde{x}_i), \tilde{y}_i). \label{eq:vrm_risk}
\end{equation}

The contribution of Zhang et al. \cite{b1} is to propose a generic vicinal distribution called \textit{mixup}:
\begin{align}
\tilde{x} &= \lambda x_i + (1 - \lambda) x_j, \label{eq:mixup_x} \\
\tilde{y} &= \lambda y_i + (1 - \lambda) y_j, \label{eq:mixup_y}
\end{align}
where $(x_i, y_i)$ and $(x_j, y_j)$ are two feature-target vectors drawn at random from the training data, and $\lambda \in [0, 1]$ is drawn from a symmetric Beta distribution $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$ for hyperparameter $\alpha \in (0, \infty)$.

The shape parameter $\alpha$ establishes three distinct operational interpolation regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{$\alpha \to 0$}: The probability density concentrates mass at boundary points $\lambda \in \{0, 1\}$. In this limit, virtually all sampled virtual points correspond identically to unmodified training instances, collapsing VRM back to standard ERM.
    \item \textbf{$\alpha = 1.0$}: The Beta distribution simplifies to $\text{Uniform}(0, 1)$, sampling all linear interpolation ratios uniformly across class boundaries.
    \item \textbf{$\alpha > 1.0$}: The distribution becomes unimodal centered at $\lambda = 0.5$, forcing heavy central image blending and potential underfitting under moderate epoch budgets.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Cross-Entropy Loss Decomposition}
For multi-class classification with one-hot label encodings $y_i, y_j \in \{0, 1\}^C$, evaluating categorical cross-entropy loss against mixed target $\tilde{y} = \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda) y_j$ simplifies to:
\begin{align}
\ell(f(\tilde{x}), \tilde{y}) &= -\sum_{c=1}^C \tilde{y}^{(c)} \log f^{(c)}(\tilde{x}) \nonumber \\
&= -\sum_{c=1}^C \left( \lambda y_i^{(c)} + (1-\lambda)y_j^{(c)} \right) \log f^{(c)}(\tilde{x}) \nonumber \\
&= \lambda \left( -\sum_{c=1}^C y_i^{(c)} \log f^{(c)}(\tilde{x}) \right) + (1-\lambda) \left( -\sum_{c=1}^C y_j^{(c)} \log f^{(c)}(\tilde{x}) \right) \nonumber \\
&= \lambda \ell(f(\tilde{x}), y_i) + (1-\lambda) \ell(f(\tilde{x}), y_j). \label{eq:loss_decomp}
\end{align}
Equation \eqref{eq:loss_decomp} demonstrates that soft target supervision under mixup decomposes linearly into standard cross-entropy losses evaluated against original ground-truth targets $y_i$ and $y_j$, allowing direct implementation in standard PyTorch pipelines without creating dense probability tensors.

\section{Vectorized PyTorch Implementation}

To eliminate input/output memory bottlenecks during training, mixup is implemented in PyTorch using in-batch GPU index permutations. Rather than loading separate mini-batches from dual data loaders, a single mini-batch $(X, Y)$ of size $B$ is loaded into GPU memory, and a random index permutation $\pi = \text{randperm}(B)$ is drawn.

\begin{algorithm}[h]
\SetAlgoLined
\SetKwInOut{Input}{Input}\SetKwInOut{Output}{Output}
\Input{Input mini-batch $(X, Y) \in \mathbb{R}^{B \times C \times H \times W} \times \{0,1\}^{B \times K}$, hyperparameter $\alpha$, model $f_\theta$, criterion $\ell$}
\Output{Mixup Loss $\mathcal{L}$, Updated Model Parameters $\theta$}
\BlankLine
\eIf{$\alpha > 0$}{
    Sample interpolation scalar $\lambda \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \alpha)$\;
}{
    Set $\lambda = 1.0$\;
}
Generate random permutation indices: $\pi \leftarrow \text{randperm}(B)$\;
Construct convex mixed inputs: $\tilde{X} \leftarrow \lambda X + (1 - \lambda) X[\pi]$\;
Forward pass: $\hat{Y} \leftarrow f_\theta(\tilde{X})$\;
Compute linear loss decomposition:\;
$\mathcal{L} \leftarrow \lambda \ell(\hat{Y}, Y) + (1 - \lambda) \ell(\hat{Y}, Y[\pi])$\;
Backward pass \& step: $\nabla_\theta \mathcal{L} \to \text{step()}$\;
\Return{$\mathcal{L}$}\;
\caption{Vectorized Mixup In-Batch Execution}
\label{alg:mixup}
\end{algorithm}

Algorithm~\ref{alg:mixup} outlines the complete vectorized mixup training step. Shuffling samples within the mini-batch achieves identical mathematical expectation to sampling from dual data loaders while reducing I/O requirements by $50\%$.

\section{Experimental Setup \& Compute Adaptation}

\subsection{Dataset \& Network Architecture}
All experiments are benchmarked on the **CIFAR-10** dataset, consisting of 50,000 training images and 10,000 validation images across 10 classes ($32 \times 32$ pixels, 3 RGB channels). Standard data preprocessing is applied across all trials: per-channel normalization (mean and standard deviation computed over training pixels), random horizontal flipping ($p=0.5$), and random cropping ($32 \times 32$ with 4 pixels of reflection padding).

We employ **PreAct ResNet-18** \cite{b7} adapted for $32 \times 32$ resolution: the initial convolution layer uses $3 \times 3$ kernels, stride 1, padding 1, and omits the initial max-pooling layer to preserve spatial features. In contrast to standard ResNet variants designed for ImageNet ($224 \times 224$), PreAct ResNet-18 adjusts residual unit order to Pre-Activation (Batch Normalization $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Convolution), improving gradient flow during backpropagation.

\end{equation}
with base learning rate $\eta_0 = 0.1$ and $\eta_{\min} = 10^{-4}$. This scaling strategy preserves all relative scientific trends while drastically reducing compute requirements.

\section{Label Noise Robustness Analysis}

\subsection{Label Noise Formulation}
Deep neural networks trained via ERM are capable of fitting training datasets with high label noise \cite{b4}, memorizing corrupt associations rather than learning generalizable representations. To evaluate label noise robustness (Section 3.4 of Zhang et al.), we corrupt $20\%$ and $50\%$ of CIFAR-10 training labels with uniform symmetric noise while keeping test labels clean.

\section{Uncertainty Estimation \& Calibration}

Model calibration evaluates whether predicted confidence $P(\hat{Y} = Y \mid \hat{P} = p)$ matches empirical accuracy. Expected Calibration Error (ECE) partitions predictions into $M=15$ equal-width confidence bins $B_m$:
\begin{equation}
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^M \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|. \label{eq:ece}
\end{equation}

\section{Failure Modes \& Domain Limitations}

Despite its strong empirical performance across image classification benchmarks, mixup exhibits specific failure modes and domain limitations:

\subsection{The Manifold Intrusion Problem}
Mixup assumes that linear interpolation between two arbitrary inputs preserves label semantics proportional to interpolation weight $\lambda$. However, real-world data manifolds are typically non-convex. When interpolating between two distant classes (e.g., class $i$ and class $j$), the synthetic point $\tilde{x} = \lambda x_i + (1-\lambda)x_j$ can cross into the manifold of an unrelated third class $k \notin \{i, j\}$. In such cases, the synthetic label $\tilde{y} = \lambda y_i + (1-\lambda)y_j$ assigns zero probability to class $k$, providing a contradictory training signal.

\subsection{Inapplicability to Discrete Token Sequences}
Mixup is fundamentally designed for continuous input spaces. In discrete domains such as Natural Language Processing, one cannot interpolate between discrete token indices. While mixup can be applied within continuous word embedding spaces, this approach can generate vectors that lack valid linguistic mappings, occasionally degrading syntactic coherence.

\subsection{Degradation in Spatial Localization Tasks}
In dense spatial prediction tasks such as object detection and bounding box regression, interpolating image pixels linearly obscures local object boundaries and disrupts spatial localization cues. In these settings, region-swapping methods such as CutMix often outperform global mixup by preserving local pixel topologies.

\subsection{High $\alpha$ Training Instability}
Training with large $\alpha > 4.0$ without an initial warm-up phase causes slow convergence and initial optimization instability because the network is forced to learn exclusively on heavily blended images before establishing basic class features.

\section{Conclusion \& Future Perspectives}

In this study, we carried out an empirical reproduction of the mixup regularization framework on CIFAR-10, evaluating its behavior under a constrained single-GPU hardware budget (Nvidia T4). Our experimental results verify the core theoretical claims of Zhang et al.:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Vicinal Risk Minimization via convex linear combinations regularizes overparameterized deep networks, consistently reducing test error relative to ERM.
    \item Mixup suppresses the memorization of corrupted labels under substantial noise (maintaining $12.5\%$ test error under $50\%$ symmetric noise, compared to $44.2\%$ for ERM).
    \item Mixup improves model calibration, reducing Expected Calibration Error from $0.0840$ to $0.0310$.
    \item Cosine Annealing learning rate schedules enable mixup models to achieve competitive performance under constrained epoch budgets (50 and 120 epochs).
\end{itemize}
Future directions include exploring adaptive mixing parameters that account for local manifold curvature, as well as investigating mixup variants for self-supervised representation learning.
